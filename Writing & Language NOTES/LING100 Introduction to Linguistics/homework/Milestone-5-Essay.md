The counselor, a native of Seoul, considered Korean—particularly Standard Korean, which is highly regarded in the Seoul/Gyeonggi region—the most salient symbol of her national linguistic identity. In contrast, English still has high instrumental value in education and employment, but is far less emotionally valuable than English; she noted that speaking English “doesn’t feel like myself.” Her testimony suggests that bilinguals can remain functionally bilingual while maintaining a clear emotional hierarchy between the languages ​​they master.

The counselor further stated that speaking Korean in the United States would immediately label her a “foreigner.” In class, she sometimes encountered some well-intentioned curiosity, but when colleagues overheard her speaking Korean, they occasionally assumed she had difficulty communicating and limited interactions to superficial topics. These experiences demonstrate how non-native accents can trigger implicit social reshaping that has nothing to do with the actual communication context.

The Korean language consists of several major dialect groups. The counselor distinguished between the Seoul/Gyeonggi, Gyeongsang, Jeolla, Gangwon, Chungcheong, and Jeju dialects, each of which has its own unique deictic meanings. Seoul dialect is the national standard dialect, widely spread and therefore considered “easy to understand”. In contrast, Gyeongsang dialect is characterized by high pitch and concise intonation, often associated with directness and energy, although outsiders sometimes characterize it as “stilted”. Gangwon dialect is slower in speech and has a more conservative vocabulary. These assessments suggest a link between linguistic features and social class and regional identity. The consultant herself uses the standard dialect in most interactions, valuing its intelligibility and institutional legitimacy. Nonetheless, she also appreciates the expressive potential of non-standard forms and opposes prescriptive attitudes that equate dialects with local languages. When asked about features that are commonly stigmatized outside of her hometown, she mentioned the sentence-final particles -dey/-daa of Gyeongsang dialect. In the Seoul dialect community, these markers may be derided as vulgar, but in their native context, they symbolize warmth and group solidarity, an example of a linguistic variable that carries negative connotations in the wider society but has positive social significance within a specific group.

Korean is learned in early childhood through natural interactions at home and in school. Formal English instruction began in third grade. This counselor could be classified as a serial bilingual because her native grammar was already very solid when she learned her second language. She said that learning English has been challenging, mainly because of the limited opportunities for authentic communication practice in Korea.

Interference from her native language was particularly prominent. The standard SOV order in Korean contrasts with the SVO order in English, causing her to frequently misuse verbs in English clauses (e.g., "I pizza ate"). The use of prepositions was equally difficult: she sometimes omitted or incorrectly selected prepositions, errors that stemmed from mapping Korean postpositional particles onto the English prepositional system. This transfer phenomenon is common in second language acquisitionbecause the grammar of the source and target languages ​​differs greatly. Korean continued to be the language that felt most natural to her as she recorded her inner thoughts and nighttime dreams.

Phonetics was the most difficult area to master. English contains fricatives /f/, /v/, /θ/ (as in think), and /ð/ (as in this), which are absent in Korean. English also retains a distinct /l/–/r/ contrast, whereas Korean has only one liquid, /ㄹ/, pronounced as an alveolar tap \[ɾ\] between vowels; this allophone is distinct from the two categories in English. Mastering the pronunciation and perception of these new segments requires building new motor habits and perceptual categories. For example, she recalled having chronic difficulties distinguishing lice from rice during childhood. The Korean phonological system favors simple CV syllables; complex consonant clusters in words like strengths often elicited intercalated vowels when she mentally analyzed them. Unstressed vowels tend to decrease during processing and practice, which increases the cognitive load required to produce these words fluently.

Morphologically, Korean is an agglutinative language: grammatical categories such as tense, aspect, modality, mood, and politeness are expressed by adding suffixes to the verb stem. For example, mek-ess-ta corresponds to "eat past tense". In contrast, English has relatively limited morphological variation, relying more on word order and auxiliary verb structure to encode similar additional information. Syntactically, Korean follows a basic SOV template and uses postpositional case markers (-i/ga for nominative, -eul/reul for accusative), allowing for considerable constituent flexibility. Meanwhile, English fixes arguments in SVO order and uses prepositions and strict syntax to mark grammatical relations. Modifiers in Korean precede nouns (ppalkan jip "red house"), and postpositions replace English prepositional phrases, for example, Canada-eseo means "from Canada"). Phonetically, Korean has three types of stops and affricates—flat, tense, and aspirated—that lack English equivalents; the two vowel groups also don’t map perfectly to each other.

This project deepened my understanding of Korean morphology, such as tense morphemes for example, the past tense -eoss-, present tense -(neun)/-n-, and the progressive marker -go iss-da. I also learned syntactic principles, including the postposition -eseo (“from,” “in/at”) and the demonstratives i (“this”) and jeo (“that”). Transliteration using the IPA allowed me to untangle segmental realization and allophones. In addition, I explored Korean dual number systems—both native Korean and Hanja Korean—and the derivation of ordinal numbers.

Learning a language that was unfamiliar to me made me place greater emphasis on systematic observation and structural analysis rather than intuition. Data acquisition was much easier than expected due to the cooperation of the consultant and scaffolding prompts, which facilitated targeted instruction, feedback, and guided analysis.

Many of the research methods used in this project—recording with native-speaker consultants, phonetic annotation based on the IPA and morphosyntactic analysis of extracted corpus—are easily transferable to the documentation of endangered languages. These techniques are crucial because they can capture and preserve original language data, both phonetics and structure, in an era when the number of speakers is declining and comprehensive descriptive language materials are scarce or even absent. The creation of such corpus is essential for the preservation of linguistic heritage.
