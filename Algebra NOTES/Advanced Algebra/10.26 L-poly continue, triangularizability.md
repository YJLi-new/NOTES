## Remarks on $p(T)$

$p(x), q(x) \in \mathbb{F}[x]$, $T: V \to V$.

1. The operators $p(T), q(T): V \to V$ satisfy $p(T)\circ q(T) = q(T)\circ p(T)$ (recall: in general $T\circ S \ne S\circ T$).
   E.g. let $p(x) = x + 1$, $q(x) = 3x^2 + 4x + 5$:
   $$p(T)\circ q(T) = (T + I)(3T\circ T + 4T + 5I) = 3T^3 + 7T^2 + 9T + 5I = r(T)$$
   where $r(x) = p(x)\cdot q(x) = 3x^3 + 7x^2 + 9x + 5$.
2. For $\dim(V) < \infty$, $m_T(x)$ always exists (已证) and $m_T(x)$ is **unique**.
   *Proof.* Suppose that $p_1(x), p_2(x)$ are polys satisfying the properties of a minimal poly, i.e. (1) $p_i(T) = \mathbf{0}_{V\to V}$, (2) $p_i(x)$ is monic, (3) $p_i(x)$ is of the smallest possible degree. (WTS: $p_1(x) = p_2(x)$.)
   Suppose that $p_1(x) \ne p_2(x)$. (3) $\Rightarrow \deg p_1 = \deg p_2$; (2) $\Rightarrow t(x) = p_1(x) - p_2(x)$ is nonzero and $\deg(t) < \deg(p_1)$.
   Remark: $t(T) = p_1(T) - p_2(T) \overset{(1)}{=} \mathbf{0} - \mathbf{0} = \mathbf{0}_{V\to V}$.
   Conclusion: let $q(x) = \frac{1}{\alpha}t(x)$, $\alpha$ the leading coeff. of $t(x)$. Then $q(x)$ satisfies (1)–(2) and has smaller degree — contradicting (3).
3. Suppose $f(x) \in \mathbb{F}[x]$. Then $f(T) = \mathbf{0}_{V\to V} \Rightarrow \deg(m_T) \le \deg(f)$ and $m_T(x) \mid f(x)$.
   *Proof.* Apply division $f(x)/m_T(x)$: $f(x) = q(x)m_T(x) + r(x)$, $\deg(r) < \deg(m_T)$.
   Then $\underbrace{f(T)}_{=\mathbf{0}} = q(T)\underbrace{m_T(T)}_{=\mathbf{0}} + r(T) \Rightarrow r(T) = \mathbf{0}_{V\to V}$.
   By minimality of $\deg(m_T)$, $r(x) = 0$, and hence $f(x) = q(x)m_T(x)$.

## Uses of the minimal polynomial

**Theorem 1 (Primary Decomposition; use of $m_T(x)$).** $T: V \to V$ is diagonalizable $\iff m_T(x) = (x - \mu_1)\cdots(x - \mu_k)$ for distinct $\mu_1,\dots,\mu_k$.

**Theorem 2 (Cayley–Hamilton; way to find $m_T(x)$).** $\chi_T(T) = \mathbf{0}_{V\to V} \overset{(\text{前 } ③)}{\iff} m_T(x) \mid \chi_T(x)$.

**E.g.**

1. $T: \mathbb{R}^4 \to \mathbb{R}^4$, $T(x) = \begin{pmatrix}1&1&0&0\\0&1&0&0\\0&0&2&0\\0&0&0&2\end{pmatrix}x$. Then $\chi_T(x) = (x-1)^2(x-2)^2$ (by MAT2040).
   By Cayley–Hamilton, $m_T(x)$ can only be:
   - deg 4: $(x-1)^2(x-2)^2$
   - deg 3: $(x-1)^2(x-2)$, $(x-2)^2(x-1)$
   - deg 2: $(x-1)^2$, $(x-1)(x-2)$, $(x-2)^2$
   - deg 1: $(x-1)$, $(x-2)$

   Checking (见题), $m_T(x) = (x-1)^2(x-2)$ $\overset{\text{Thm 1}}{\Rightarrow}$ $T$ is not diagonalizable.
2. $T: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^2$, $T(x) = \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix}x$.
   (MAT2040) $\Rightarrow \chi_T(x) = x^2 + 1$; (C.-H.) $\Rightarrow m_T(x) = x^2 + 1$; (Thm 1) $\Rightarrow T$ is not diagonalizable.
3. (2') $T: \mathbb{C}^2 \to \mathbb{C}^2$, same matrix. (MAT2040) $\Rightarrow \chi_T(x) = (x + i)(x - i)$; (C.-H.) $\Rightarrow m_T(x) \in \{x + i,\ x - i,\ (x+i)(x-i)\}$, and only $(x+i)(x-i)$ works.
   (Thm 1) $\Rightarrow T$ is diagonalizable over $\mathbb{C}$.

## Triangularizability & C.-H. Theorem

**Def.** $T: V \to V$ is linear, $\dim(V) = n < \infty$.

- $T$ is **diagonalizable** $\Leftarrow \exists$ basis $B$ of $V$ s.t. $T_{BB} = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n)$.
- (weaker) $T$ is **triangularizable** ($\triangle$-able) $\Leftarrow \exists$ basis $B$ s.t. $T_{BB} = \begin{pmatrix}\lambda_1 & & *\\ & \ddots & \\ 0 & & \lambda_n\end{pmatrix}$ (upper triangular).

**Remark.** $T: V \to V$ is diag.
$\iff \exists$ basis $B = \{v_1,\dots,v_n\}$ of $V$ s.t. $T_{BB} = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n)$
$\iff T(v_i) = \underbrace{\mathbf{0} + \cdots + \mathbf{0}}_{i-1} + \lambda_iv_i + \underbrace{\mathbf{0} + \cdots + \mathbf{0}}_{n-i}$
$\iff v_i$ are eigenvectors of $T$ with eigenvalue $\lambda_i$
$\iff B$ is a basis of eigenvectors (def. of diag. in MAT2040).

**Theorem.** $T: V \to V$ is $\triangle$-able $\iff \chi_T(x) = (x - \lambda_1)(x - \lambda_2)\cdots(x - \lambda_n)$ (here $\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n$ are not necessarily distinct; up to sign $(-1)^n$).

E.g. $T(x) = \begin{pmatrix}1&1&0&0\\0&1&0&0\\0&0&2&0\\0&0&0&2\end{pmatrix}x$ is $\triangle$-able $\Leftarrow \chi_T(x) = (x-1)^2(x-2)^2 = (x-1)(x-1)(x-2)(x-2)$.

**Prop. 1.** $T$ is $\triangle$-able $\Rightarrow \chi_T(x) = (-1)^n(x - \lambda_1)\cdots(x - \lambda_n)$.

*Proof.* Let $B$ be a basis of $V$ s.t. $T_{BB}$ is upper triangular with diagonal $\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n$. Then
$\chi_T(x) = \det(T_{BB} - xI) = \det\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_1 - x & & *\\ & \ddots & \\ 0 & & \lambda_n - x\end{pmatrix} \overset{\text{MAT2040}}{=} (\lambda_1 - x)\cdots(\lambda_n - x)$.

**Prop. 2.** $\chi_T(x) = (x - \lambda_1)\cdots(x - \lambda_n) \Rightarrow T: V \to V$ is $\triangle$-able.

*Proof.* By $\chi_T(x) = (x - \lambda_1)\cdots(x - \lambda_n)$ & MAT2040, $\exists w \in V\setminus\{0\}$ an eigenvector of $T$ with eigenvalue $\lambda_1$.
Consider $W := \operatorname{Span}\{w\} \le V$. Then $W$ is a $T$-invariant subspace: take $\alpha w \in W$; $T(\alpha w) = (\alpha\lambda_1)w \in W \Rightarrow T(W) \subseteq W$.
(HW5) Then $T|_W: W \to W$ and $\bar T: V/W \to V/W$, and ∃ bases $A$ of $V$, $\mathcal{C} = \{w\}$ of $W$, $\mathcal{D}$ of $V/W$, s.t.

$$T_{AA} = \begin{pmatrix}(T|_W)_{\mathcal{CC}} & *\\ 0 & \bar T_{\mathcal{DD}}\end{pmatrix}$$

with $(T|_W)_{\mathcal{CC}}$ of size $1\times 1$ and $\bar T_{\mathcal{DD}}$ of size $(n-1)\times(n-1)$; continue by induction.
