## Theorem (Riesz Representation)

$(V, \langle\,,\rangle)$: an inner product space.

$$\phi: V \to V^*,\quad v \mapsto \phi_v,\qquad \phi_v: V \to \mathbb{F},\ \phi_v(w) := \langle v, w\rangle$$

Then:

- (a) $\phi_v \in V^*$;
- (b) $\phi$ is injective;
- (c) $\phi$ is an $\mathbb{R}$-linear transformation, i.e. $\forall a, b \in \mathbb{R}$, $\phi(av + bv') = a\phi(v) + b\phi(v')$ (and $\phi(iv) \ne i\phi(v)$ in the complex case).

*Proof.*

- (a) $\phi_v(\alpha w + \beta w') = \langle v, \alpha w + \beta w'\rangle = \langle v, \alpha w\rangle + \langle v, \beta w'\rangle = \alpha\langle v, w\rangle + \beta\langle v, w'\rangle = \alpha\phi_v(w) + \beta\phi_v(w')$ ($\alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{F}$).
- (b) Suppose $k \in \ker(\phi)$ ⇒ $\phi(k) = \phi_k = \mathbf{0}_{V^*}$ ⇒ $\forall w \in V$, $\phi_k(w) = \mathbf{0}_{V^*}(w) = 0$ ⇒ $\forall w \in V$, $\langle k, w\rangle = 0$. Take $w = k$: $\langle k, k\rangle = 0 \overset{\text{positive definite}}{\iff} k = \mathbf{0}$.
- (c) (WTS: $\phi(av + bv') = a\phi(v) + b\phi(v')$.) Take $\forall w \in V$.
  LHS: $\phi(av + bv')(w) = \langle av + bv', w\rangle = \langle av, w\rangle + \langle bv', w\rangle = a\langle v, w\rangle + b\langle v', w\rangle$ (real $a, b$) $=$ RHS.

**Corollary.** $V$ is an $\mathbb{R}$-inner product space and $\dim_{\mathbb{R}}(V) < \infty$ ⇒ $\phi$ is an isomorphism.
*Proof.* $\dim(V^*) = \dim(V) < \infty$ and $\phi: V \to V^*$ is inj. ⇒ $\phi$ is bij.

关于 $\mathbb{C}$-inner product space $V$: $\phi$ 不是 $\mathbb{C}$-linear trans.
To remedy: treat $V$ as an $\mathbb{R}$-inner product space. E.g. $V = \mathbb{C}^n = \operatorname{Span}_{\mathbb{C}}\{e_1,\dots,e_n\}$; at the same time treat $V = \mathbb{C}^n$ as an $\mathbb{R}$-vector space:

$$v = \begin{pmatrix}a_1 + b_1i\\ \vdots\\ a_n + b_ni\end{pmatrix} = a_1e_1 + b_1(ie_1) + \cdots + a_ne_n + b_n(ie_n) \Rightarrow v \in \operatorname{Span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{e_1, ie_1, \dots, e_n, ie_n\}$$

(Whiteboard) In general, if $\dim_{\mathbb{C}}(V) = n$ with basis $V = \operatorname{Span}\{v_1,\dots,v_n\}$, then $V_{\mathbb{R}}$ is also an $\mathbb{R}$-vector space of $\dim_{\mathbb{R}}(V_{\mathbb{R}}) = 2n$ with basis $\{v_1, iv_1, \dots, v_n, iv_n\}$. Similarly, a $\mathbb{C}$-inner product space $(V, \langle,\rangle_{\mathbb{C}})$ can be seen as an $\mathbb{R}$-inner product space $(V_{\mathbb{R}}, \langle,\rangle_{\mathbb{R}})$. Then $\phi: V_{\mathbb{R}} \to (V_{\mathbb{R}})^*$ becomes an isomorphism of $\mathbb{R}$-vector spaces, i.e. $\phi: V \to V^*$ is a bijection (but not $\mathbb{C}$-linear).

## Adjoint Operator

**Lemma.** Let $(V, \langle\,,\rangle)$ be a finite-dimensional inner product space over $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$, and $T: V \to V$ a linear operator. Then ∃ **unique** $a_v \in V$ s.t. $\langle a_v, w\rangle = \langle v, T(w)\rangle$, $\forall w \in V$.

*Proof.* Consider $\gamma \in V^*$, $\gamma = \phi_v\circ T$, i.e. $\gamma: w \mapsto T(w) \mapsto \langle v, T(w)\rangle \in \mathbb{F}$.
(Whiteboard) By Riesz Representation there's a bijection $\phi: V \to V^*$ ⇒ unique $a_v \in V$ so that $a_v \mapsto \phi_{a_v} = \gamma$, i.e. $\phi_{a_v}(w) = \gamma(w)$ $\forall w \in V$ ⇒ $\langle a_v, w\rangle = \langle v, T(w)\rangle$. ∎

### Adjoint map

**Def.** For $(V, \langle\,,\rangle)$, the adjoint map of $T$ is $T^*: V \to V$, $v \mapsto a_v$, $T^*(v) := a_v$, i.e.

$$\forall v, w \in V,\quad \langle T^*(v), w\rangle = \langle v, T(w)\rangle$$

(Whiteboard example) $(\mathbb{R}^n, \langle\,,\rangle)$ dot product, $T: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^n$ given by $T(x) = Ax$ ($A \in M_{n\times n}(\mathbb{R})$). What is $T^*$? A: $T^*(v) = A^tv$ ($A^t$ = transpose of $A$).
Remark: often people use the same notation $T^*$ to denote (i) $T^*: W^* \to V^*$ (transpose of $T$) and (ii) $T^*: V \to V$ (adjoint of $T$). In our first example, transpose = adjoint, but not true in general.

**Remark.** transpose of $T$: $T^*: W^* \to V^*$ $\ne$ adjoint of $T$: $T^*: V \to V$.

**E.g. 2.** $\mathbb{C}^n$, $\langle v, w\rangle = (\bar v_1,\dots,\bar v_n)(w_1,\dots,w_n)^T = \bar v^tw$; $T(x) = Ax$ with $A \in M_{n\times n}(\mathbb{C})$.
Claim: $T^*(v) = \bar A^tv$.
LHS: $\langle T^*(v), w\rangle = \overline{(\bar A^tv)}^tw = \bar v^t\overline{(\bar A^t)}^tw = \bar v^tAw$.
RHS: $\langle v, T(w)\rangle = \bar v^t(Aw) =$ LHS.

**Prop.** The adjoint map $T^*: V \to V$ is a linear transformation.

*Proof.* (WTS: $T^*(\alpha v + \beta v') = \alpha T^*(v) + \beta T^*(v')$, i.e. WTS $T^*(\alpha v + \beta v') - (\alpha T^*(v) + \beta T^*(v')) = \mathbf{0}$.) Consider, $\forall w \in V$:

$$\begin{aligned}
&\langle T^*(\alpha v + \beta v') - (\alpha T^*(v) + \beta T^*(v')), w\rangle\\
&= \langle T^*(\alpha v + \beta v'), w\rangle - \langle\alpha T^*(v) + \beta T^*(v'), w\rangle\\
&= \langle\alpha v + \beta v', T(w)\rangle - \bar\alpha\langle T^*(v), w\rangle - \bar\beta\langle T^*(v'), w\rangle\\
&= \bar\alpha\langle v, T(w)\rangle + \bar\beta\langle v', T(w)\rangle - \bar\alpha\langle v, T(w)\rangle - \bar\beta\langle v', T(w)\rangle = 0
\end{aligned}$$

Since this is 0 $\forall w \in V$ and the form is positive definite ⇒ nondegenerate, $T^*(\alpha v + \beta v') - (\alpha T^*(v) + \beta T^*(v')) = \mathbf{0}$.
