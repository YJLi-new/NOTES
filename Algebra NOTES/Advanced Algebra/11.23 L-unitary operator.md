## Unitary operators

**Def.** $(V, \langle\,,\rangle)$ an inner product space, $V$ a $\mathbb{C}$-vector space. $T: V \to V$ (operator) is **unitary** $\iff \forall v, w \in V$, $\langle T(v), T(w)\rangle = \langle v, w\rangle$.

**Remark.** This holds ⇒ $T$ preserves the norm of the vector $v \in V$, i.e. $\forall v \in V$, $\|T(v)\| = \|v\|$.

**Prop.** $T$ is unitary $\iff T^*\circ T = I$.

*Proof.*
(⇒) Let $\forall x \in V$; then $\forall y \in V$:
$\langle(T^*T - I)(x), y\rangle = \langle T^*T(x), y\rangle - \langle x, y\rangle = \langle T(x), T(y)\rangle - \langle x, y\rangle = 0$.
Nondegeneracy of $\langle\,,\rangle$ ⇒ $(T^*T - I)(x) = \mathbf{0}$, $\forall x \in V$ ⇒ $T^*T - I$ is the zero operator.
(⇐) $\forall x \in V$: $T^*T = I$ ⇒ $T^*T(x) = I(x) = x$ ⇒ $\forall y \in V$, $\langle T^*T(x), y\rangle = \langle x, y\rangle$, and the LHS $= \langle T(x), T(y)\rangle$.

**E.g.** ($V = \mathbb{C}^n$, $\langle\,,\rangle$.) Recall: $T(x) = Ax$ ⇒ $T^*(x) = \bar A^tx$. Then $T$ is unitary $\iff \bar A^t\cdot A = I_{n\times n}$.
More explicitly, write $A = (u_1 \mid u_2 \mid \cdots \mid u_n)$; then $\bar A^tA = I_{n\times n}$ implies

$$\begin{pmatrix}\bar u_1^t\\ \vdots\\ \bar u_n^t\end{pmatrix}(u_1 \mid \cdots \mid u_n) = \begin{pmatrix}\langle u_1, u_1\rangle & \cdots & \langle u_1, u_n\rangle\\ \vdots & \langle u_i, u_j\rangle & \vdots\\ \langle u_n, u_1\rangle & \cdots & \langle u_n, u_n\rangle\end{pmatrix} = I$$

Therefore the columns of $A = (u_1 \mid \cdots \mid u_n)$ form an orthonormal basis.

## Unitary matrix

**Def.** An $n\times n$ complex matrix $A$ is unitary $\iff \bar A^tA = I \iff$ the columns form an orthonormal basis of $\mathbb{C}^n$.

**Lemma 1.** A unitary operator $T: V \to V$: every eigenvalue $\lambda \in \mathbb{C}$ of $T$ has $|\lambda| = 1$ ($|a + bi| = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$).

*Proof.* Let $T(w) = \lambda w$ (eigen). Then $\langle T(w), T(w)\rangle = \langle w, w\rangle$ ⇒ $\langle\lambda w, \lambda w\rangle = \bar\lambda\lambda\langle w, w\rangle = \langle w, w\rangle$. $\langle w, w\rangle \ne 0$ ⇒ $\bar\lambda\lambda = 1$ ⇒ $|\lambda|^2 = 1$ ⇒ $|\lambda| = 1$. (i.e. $\exists\theta \in \mathbb{R}$, $\lambda = e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta$.)

**Lemma 2.** $T: V \to V$ is a unitary operator and $U \le V$ is $T$-invariant ⇒ $U \le V$ is $T^{-1} = T^*$-invariant.

*Proof.* $T^*T = I$ ⇒ $T$ is invertible and $T^{-1} = T^*$ ⇒ 0 is not an eigenvalue of $T$, i.e. $\chi_T(0) \ne 0$.
Write $\chi_T(x) = x^n + a_{n-1}x^{n-1} + \cdots + a_1x + a_0$; then $a_0 \ne 0$.
By C.-H., $\chi_T(T) = T^n + a_{n-1}T^{n-1} + \cdots + a_1T + a_0I = \mathbf{0}_{V\to V}$

$$\Rightarrow I = -\frac{1}{a_0}T^n - \frac{a_{n-1}}{a_0}T^{n-1} - \cdots - \frac{a_1}{a_0}T = \Big(-\frac{1}{a_0}T^{n-1} - \cdots - \frac{a_1}{a_0}I\Big)T\ \Rightarrow\ T^{-1} = -\frac{1}{a_0}T^{n-1} - \cdots - \frac{a_1}{a_0}I$$

$T^{-1} = T^*$ ⇒ $T^* = -\frac{1}{a_0}T^{n-1} - \cdots - \frac{a_1}{a_0}I$.
(WTS: $U \le V$ is $T^{-1}$-invariant.) Take $\forall u \in U$; then $T^{-1}(u) = \cdots$ (每项都 $\in U$) ⇒ $T^{-1}(u) \in U$.

**Lemma 3.** $T: V \to V$ is unitary and $U \le V$ is $T$-invariant ⇒ $U^\perp \le V$ is $T$-invariant.

*Proof.* Let $\forall w \in U^\perp$, $\forall u \in U$: $\langle u, T(w)\rangle = \langle T^*(u), w\rangle$; Lemma 2 ⇒ $T^*(u) \in U$ ⇒ $= 0$ (since $w \in U^\perp$) ⇒ $T(w) \in U^\perp$.

**Theorem 1.** $(V, \langle\,,\rangle)$ is finite-dimensional, $T: V \to V$ is a unitary operator ⇒ $V$ has an orthonormal basis $B$ of eigenvectors.

(Whiteboard) *Proof.* Use induction on $\dim(V)$. If $\dim(V) = 1$, easy. Suppose the theorem holds for all vector spaces $V_k$ with $\dim(V_k) = k$ and all unitary operators $T_k: V_k \to V_k$. Then consider $V_{k+1}$ with $\dim(V_{k+1}) = k + 1$ and $T: V_{k+1} \to V_{k+1}$ unitary.
Since $V_{k+1}$ is a $\mathbb{C}$-vector space, any operator $T: V_{k+1} \to V_{k+1}$ has an eigenvector $u \in V_{k+1}$. Then let $U := \operatorname{Span}\{u\}$ ($\dim U = 1$). So we have:
- $U^\perp$ has dimension $k$, since $\dim(U) + \dim(U^\perp) = \dim(V_{k+1})$;
- $U^\perp$ is $T$-invariant by Lemma 3.

$T|_{U^\perp}: U^\perp \to U^\perp$ satisfies $\langle T|_{U^\perp}(v), T|_{U^\perp}(v')\rangle = \langle T(v), T(v')\rangle = \langle v, v'\rangle$, i.e. it is unitary. Take $V_k = U^\perp$ and $T_k = T|_{U^\perp}$. By induction ∃ orthonormal basis $\{v_2,\dots,v_{k+1}\}$ of $U^\perp$ s.t. $T|_{U^\perp}(v_i) = T(v_i) = \lambda_iv_i$ (i.e. $v_i$ are eigenvectors of $T$). Then $\left\{\frac{u}{\|u\|}, v_2, \dots, v_{k+1}\right\}$ is an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors of $T$ in $V$.

By Thm 1, we have $T_{BB} = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_{k+1})$.

**Corollary.** $A \in M_{n\times n}(\mathbb{C})$ is a unitary matrix ⇒ ∃ unitary matrix $P$ s.t. $P^{-1}AP = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n)$, $\forall i$, $|\lambda_i| = 1$.

*Proof.* Let $(V = \mathbb{C}^n, \langle\,,\rangle)$, $\mathcal{E} = \{e_1,\dots,e_n\}$ the usual basis of $V = \mathbb{C}^n$, and consider $T: V \to V$, $T(x) = Ax$. Observations:
1. $T_{\mathcal{EE}} = A$;
2. $A$ is a unitary matrix ⇒ $T$ is a unitary operator;
3. Thm 1 ⇒ ∃ orthonormal basis $B$ consisting of eigenvectors of $T$, i.e. $T_{BB} = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n)$.

Note that $T_{BB} = \mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{E}B}^{-1}T_{\mathcal{EE}}\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{E}B}$ with $\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{E}B} = (b_1 \mid \cdots \mid b_n)$. Therefore $\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n) = \mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{E}B}^{-1}A\,\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{E}B}$, where $\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{E}B}$ is a unitary matrix (its columns are an orthonormal basis).
