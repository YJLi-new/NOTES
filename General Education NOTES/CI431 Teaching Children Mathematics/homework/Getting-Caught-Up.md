> In the article pp 1-8, Zager highlights a disconnect: mathematicians describe math as playful and creative, but students call it boring or humiliating. The problem is not math itself but how we teach it. Traditional instruction emphasized memorizing one "right" method and prioritizing speed over understanding, training students into passive obedience. A key takeaway is that teachers need to reconnect with what it feels like to do mathematics, not just deliver procedures. I wonder how we rebuild positive math identities for students who have already internalized years of negative experiences, and how we balance covering content with the slow, exploratory sense-making Zager describes.

1)  CRA Progression (Concrete-Representational-Abstract). Moves students from manipulatives to drawings to symbols. Struggling students are often asked to work abstractly before they have a conceptual foundation. Returning to concrete models rebuilds that foundation.

2)  Tiered Question Prompts. Same core problem, different scaffolding levels (simpler numbers, sentence starters, partially completed models). All students engage with grade-level ideas at an appropriate entry point.

3)  Discourse Support (sentence frames, think-pair-share). Many struggling students have good math intuition but difficulty verbalizing it. Frames like "My strategy was to \_\_" give language scaffolds that help them participate in math talk, especially important for ELLs and students with language processing differences.

- One main takeaway from the Number Sense activity segment is that simple games can make children’s mathematical thinking visible. These activities are not just practice; they help reveal whether students are using one-to-one counting, subitizing, comparing quantities, composing and decomposing numbers, and thinking with benchmarks like 5 and 10. I also noticed that the activities create natural opportunities for students to explain strategies, which helps the teacher assess number sense without relying only on written answers.
