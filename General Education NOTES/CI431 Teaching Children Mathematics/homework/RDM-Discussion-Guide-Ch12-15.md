**Discussion Script — Chapters 12–15**

*Rethinking Disability and Mathematics · Rachel Lambert*

Chapter 12 — Supporting Relationships & Community in Math Class

> **►** *Ask everyone to open Chapter 12. The brownies problem is on the board.*

Chapter 12 drops us into Ms. Rey’s fifth-grade room in a Title I school — twenty-eight kids, five with IEPs, most Latino or Latina, more than half multilingual. And one detail I don’t want us to slide past: her school is NOT an inclusive school. Most students with disabilities there spend the day in separate, self-contained classrooms. Ms. Rey fought to bring two autistic students, Oscar and Ash, into her class full-time. So everything we’re about to admire exists because one teacher pushed for it.

The math is the problem on the board: four children share thirteen brownies equally. Take thirty seconds and solve it however you like.

> **►** *~30 seconds, then point people to the three student papers.*

Now look at how the ten-year-olds did it — Figure 12.2 is Ash’s, Figure 12.3 is Daniel’s, Figure 12.4 is Kelani’s. Ash direct-models: draws the kids, deals out whole brownies, cuts the last into fourths. Kelani has three labeled strategies. And Daniel divides twelve by four in his head, then writes the fraction upside down — on a worksheet that reads as ‘wrong,’ but his drawing proves he understands completely.

In interviews, every student said this class felt different from past math classes. Here are two of them, in their own words:

**Read aloud — student voices:**

> *“Because I thought about how I would get the problem wrong or right and I thought I was kinda like in trouble or something when I was getting the problems wrong.”*
>
> *“She makes us feel comfortable, more comfortable in class, like she helps us get our confidence, to be able to talk ’cause some people like, when they mess up, they laugh at them.”*

And here Lambert refuses the easy story — because the earlier teachers were kind too.

> **►** *Point to the section heading “Time and Space for Mathematical Thinking.”*

**Read aloud, slowly:**

> *“It is important to know that the teachers before Ms. Rey were also good teachers. … I do not think those other teachers felt angry or upset when a student had a wrong answer. So why were the kids convinced that those previous teachers disapproved of their wrong answers? What did Ms. Rey do differently?”*

Her answer is three deliberate moves. One: real time and freedom to think. Two: she reframes what math is. When Oscar miscounts, she doesn’t correct him — she asks him to share his ‘change in thinking.’ She asks the class for their ‘favorite mistake of the day’ and draws a heart next to it. Even Toby, her highest scorer, models it: wrong in an argument, he just smiles and says ‘I changed my thinking,’ and gets a laugh. And on time, she tells a class sent to recess:

**Read aloud, slowly:**

> *“Mathematicians do not hurry, instead they are thoughtful.”* — Ms. Rey

She’s blunt about why the feelings matter — and she has the data: students with learning disabilities have math anxiety at twice the rate of other students.

**Read aloud, slowly:**

> *“Kids think harder when they care! We all do.”*

Move three: interdependence. The clearest moment is when the table can’t hear Oscar, a boy who barely speaks:

**Read aloud, slowly:**

> *“Instead of asking Oscar to speak louder, she asked Kelani, “Can you hear Oscar?” When Kelani shook her head no, Ms. Rey asked her, “So what can you do about it?” Kelani stood up and knelt next to Oscar.”*

Two last details that are the soul of the chapter. Oscar’s IEP calls him ‘nonverbal’; Ms. Rey calls the same boy ‘shy.’ And one day Kelani quietly rewrites a story problem to be about cats, just so Ash — who loves cats — will engage. It works. The children have started accommodating each other.

Questions to raise

1.  **Nobody was ever actually ‘in trouble’ for a wrong answer, and those earlier teachers were good, kind teachers. So where does that fear come from? What are the invisible ways an ordinary, well-run math classroom teaches children that wrong answers are dangerous?**

> **►** *Ask it, then wait a full 8 seconds before rescuing it.*

Related to that — later in the chapter, Rita has an A in seventh-grade math, but after being sorted into an unexplained ‘middle’ group she tells the researcher, ‘I’m a middle.’ Lambert says we broadcast who is ‘good at math’ through nonverbal cues — how we group, who we call on, who gets the challenge task.

1.  **What’s a cue like that you absorbed as a student, or watched a kid absorb? And is there any way to group students that doesn’t send these messages?**

Chapter 13 — Connecting Multiple Representations

> **►** *Ask everyone to turn to Chapter 13. Figure 13.1 is Kit’s photo; the task is in the “Try It” box.*

Chapter 13 moves us to New York City, into Kit Golan’s sixth-grade classroom. Kit uses both he and they pronouns — the book uses they, so I will too — and Kit is a teacher with ADHD, who designs from their own margins. Their starting problem is one every teacher knows: Kit noticed they dominated the talk, and the same few students answered everything. Kit’s diagnosis is sharp — whoever is doing the talking is doing the thinking.

The vehicle is an Instructional Routine called Connecting Representations. Look at the ‘Try It’ box: three equations — A, three x equals six; B, six x equals twelve; C, six x plus three equals fifteen — and only two double number lines. Kit opens by naming a goal, and notice it’s a thinking goal, not a content one:

**Read aloud, slowly:**

> *“Today, we will be connecting double number lines to equations so that we can think like mathematicians do, and use mathematical structure to connect two things that look different.”* — Kit Golan, opening the routine
>
> **►** *Give ~30 seconds to match them. The equation with no partner is A.*

Now watch the choreography — none of it is casual. Silent think time, then partner talk with sentence starters on the screen. Kit picks two pairs and rehearses with them first, so nobody presents cold. One student talks while the other points — no marker yet. Only afterward does Kit annotate the connections in color.

> **►** *Point to Figures 13.2 and 13.3 — the ‘chunks’ circled in color.*

And the point of all that structure is not control — it’s cognitive room:

**Read aloud, slowly:**

> *“Each Instructional Routine uses a consistent structure with the goal of freeing up students’ mental resources to think mathematically instead of thinking, “Wait, what do I do next?””*

Even the numbers are engineered — the same three and six repeat, so students can’t ‘number grab’ and must read structure instead. That’s Standard for Mathematical Practice 7. And Lambert argues this connecting work isn’t an add-on; it’s the nature of the subject:

**Read aloud, slowly:**

> *“Mathematics is fundamentally multimodal. Mathematics is about how numbers and space operate.”*

Why sentence starters, though? Some people hear ‘sentence frames’ and think remedial. Here’s Kit, on their own brain:

**Read aloud, slowly:**

> *“…having a combination of ADHD and perfectionist tendencies, getting started sometimes feels impossible, even with just saying a sentence about a math problem. A sentence frame can give permission to get started, and help students know that they are on the right track.”* — Kit Golan

And the students felt exactly what the structure was doing. Kit asked them, and here are their own words:

**Read aloud — student voices:**

> *“We do lots of talking with your partners and they help me catch my mistakes in problems, and that isn’t something I did often in elementary school.”*
>
> *“I like that instead of all our heads facing the teacher, they were turned to each other. That felt really good.”*
>
> *“Mr. Golan doesn’t talk all class like some teachers.”*
>
> *“It helped me understand stuff and breaks apart the hard stuff.”*

Kit’s own summary of why the small segments work:

**Read aloud, slowly:**

> *“There’s no huge chunk of time where students have a chance to get off task, or to fall behind everybody else, right? Because we’re all sort of doing the same thing. But it’s in these structured, smaller moments.”* — Kit Golan

Questions to raise

One setup: Lambert holds this routine against the formal definition of explicit instruction — model, guided practice, check, feedback, independent practice — and says Kit does every element except the first. Kit never shows anyone how to solve it. She calls it ‘guided strategy development.’

1.  **Ms. Rey’s room ran on freedom; Kit’s runs on script. Lambert claims both are sense-making classrooms, and that Kit’s structure is exactly what lets marginalized students in. Do you buy that? Where is the line between structure that frees thinking and structure that quietly replaces it?**

2.  **Kit designs for ADHD, multilingual, and LD students and argues it helps everyone. Do you buy that ‘what’s good for the margins is good for all’? Can you think of a support for one group that might get in another group’s way?**

Chapter 14 — Rethinking Assessment for Equity

> **►** *Ask everyone to find Figures 14.2 and 14.4 — Franco’s and Jeremy’s second assessments.*

Chapter 14 opens with Lambert herself getting assessment wrong. She’s been building multiplication with Franco and Jeremy out of towers of cubes, and she’s confident they’ve grown, so she re-gives the original paper test. Figure 14.2 is Franco — one out of six up to five out of six. Figure 14.4 is Jeremy — one answer, otherwise blank, legs jittering. Then she cuts a blank test into strips, writes ‘2 towers of 4’ next to 2 times 4, and the boys invent ‘tower talk’ — and Jeremy solves everything. A week later, with blocks on the table (Figure 14.6), he scores five out of six. The knowledge was there the whole time.

> **►** *Point to the Think About It box under Figure 14.6.*

The book asks it there directly: ‘So what does Jeremy know about multiplication?’ His three tests say almost nothing, almost nothing, almost everything — same child, same two weeks. Lambert’s thesis:

**Read aloud, slowly:**

> *“The purpose of assessment is for us to learn what children know rather than what they don’t know.”*

And the stakes are emotional, not technical. Two girls with IEPs describe what a test feels like:

**Read aloud — student voices:**

> *“I would understand, but I’ll get too nervous and forget something. . . . Cause when you are kind of nervous, you, forget things . . . it is just like, I should know this, wait, I forgot about this, should I remember? I just like get kind of nervous.”* — Ana
>
> *“For some reason on tests I tend to like, panic and then if I like study something I for . . . it is like I blank out completely. So, with tests, I don’t test well.”* — Desi

It isn’t only struggling students — Mr. Jay watches his strongest kids do the same:

**Read aloud, slowly:**

> *“My kids just freeze and blank out under this pressure of this test—they’re anxious and they can’t think and there’s this huge roadblock for them. . . . I mean, that’s fear—it’s fight or flight where the brain turns off.”* — Mr. Jay

The research names it — the ‘affective drop,’ where anxiety eats the working memory you need to think. Lambert’s alternative is dynamic assessment, and this is its philosophical core:

**Read aloud, slowly:**

> *“In the Disability Rights Movement, there have been decades of critique of the idea that knowledge or competence should be assessed alone, focused on deficits. … It is not about what the child can do without tools because tools are part of mathematics.”*
>
> **►** *Point to the heading “Rethinking Tests and Quizzes.”*

Mr. Jay makes his tests collaborative — random groups of three, each writing on their own paper, but free to talk — with retakes on anything, and a resource-room option most students chose to leave behind. Why he does it:

**Read aloud, slowly:**

> *“I think at this level, a math test should be a learning opportunity rather than something that will bring a kid down. I mean, a kid will feel like, oh now I’m an F, you know. So I feel like if I can make it a learning opportunity, I let kids take group tests.”* — Mr. Jay

And his students agreed. In a focus group, they said group tests beat solo tests because you:

**Read aloud — student voices:**

> *“Feel more confident in a group.”*
>
> *“\[Feel\] secure.”*
>
> *“Sometimes I forget, they remind me, it is like backup.”*
>
> *“If you don’t know something the people around you might know it, better together.”*
>
> *“Your group has to agree on it.”*

Questions to raise

I’ll play the skeptic — the department chair, or a worried parent.

1.  **‘If Jeremy can only multiply with blocks in his hands, he can’t really multiply. Group tests and retakes inflate grades and hide what a kid can do alone.’ Lambert’s reply is that ‘alone, silent, no tools’ is one arbitrary format, not a law of nature — tools are part of math. So what should a math grade actually certify? And is there anything a solo, closed-book test measures that we genuinely need?**

2.  **Mr. Jay’s tests are humane, but his students still sit the high-stakes solo state exam — the chapter opens with his strongest student frozen over sixteen minus eight. If we build humane assessment in our rooms while the machinery outside stays the same, are we preparing our students or sheltering them? What do we owe them before test day?**

Chapter 15 — Reimagining Math Goals in IEPs

> **►** *Ask everyone to turn to Figure 15.2 — Suzanne’s chart, “What do you know about 3/8?”*

The book could have ended on a warm classroom story. Instead it ends on paperwork — the IEP goal — because that’s where philosophy becomes legally binding text. Suzanne Huerta opens her fifth-grade special education class with one question — ‘What do you know about three-eighths?’ — and Figure 15.2 shows students with disabilities producing genuinely relational understanding of fractions.

But her teaching has moved to sense-making while her students’ IEP goals still read like the ones she inherited — the typical computational goal Lambert quotes:

**Read aloud, slowly:**

> *“By (date), when given 25 multiplication double digit by single digit numbers, the student will multiply 1-digit numbers by 2-digit numbers using the standard algorithm with 80% accuracy in 4 out of 5 trials.”*

To collect data on goals like that, she has to pull kids OUT of real problem solving and hand them worksheets — and the kids complain. So she rewrites the goal around Standard for Mathematical Practice 1, make sense of problems and persevere:

**Read aloud, slowly:**

> *“By \[date\], when given a story problem \[whole numbers or a fraction\], student will demonstrate use of some of these three strategies \[restating the problem in their own words, creating a representation, and revising their strategy when needed\] to make sense of a problem and persevere in solving the problem in \[a number of\] trials as measured by teacher evaluation of student work/data collection.”* — Suzanne’s revised goal

And the rewrite changed her relationship to the whole enterprise:

**Read aloud, slowly:**

> *“I feel like, for once, I am enjoying working on a goal. Usually in the past I would hand a worksheet to them, give it to me when you are done, then I would find the percentage they got correct. But now I feel like I am developing relationships with my students, not just what they do or do not know, but how they know it.”* — Suzanne Huerta

Now the stakes, in one story. Paulo Tan is a former math teacher, now a Disability Studies professor — and the father of a boy with a disability. Here is his account of his own son’s IEP meeting:

**Read aloud, slowly:**

> *“The vision is often lost in the process of developing achievable short term and discrete goals. This disconnection is often caused by the hyper-focus on remediating skill deficits. \[T\]he lead special educator teacher felt it was important for one of the IEP mathematics goals to have my son, who was in 5th grade, correctly identify one-digit numbers in a field of two. … I expressed to the teacher, that my son was way beyond identifying and recognizing numbers. … Nevertheless, the argument went back and forth for some time as they were unconvinced of my claims.”* — Paulo Tan (2017)

**Read aloud, slowly:**

> *“If we can align math IEP goals with our visions of equitable mathematics, IEP goals can shift from being a barrier to being a tool for equity.”*

Lambert’s guiding advice comes from Bateman and Linden — choose goals by how much access they buy:

**Read aloud, slowly:**

> *“Don’t clutter IEPs with detailed goals and objectives for all the content standards in the general curriculum. Instead, focus on the accommodations and adjustments an individual child needs for appropriate access to participation in the general curriculum. Goals should be prioritized and deal with large, important areas.”* — Bateman & Linden (2006)

She also shows a small UDL rewrite that changes who can even meet a goal — from ‘Tell and write time in hours and half-hours using analog and digital clocks’ to ‘Communicate the time…,’ so students who use AAC devices are included.

> **►** *Point to Table 15.2 — the MATHS checklist (Measurable, Ambitious, Toward access, High-leverage, Stakeholders).*

She walks MATHS through Jose, a real former student. Watch his inherited goal become her rewrite:

**Read aloud — Jose’s goal, before and after:**

> *“Given a set of numbers, Jose will solve two-digit subtraction problems with regrouping with 80% accuracy in trials.”* — inherited goal
>
> *“Given a fraction, Jose will generate two equivalent fractions and explain why the fractions are equivalent.”* — Lambert’s rewrite

Her bold call: don’t spend the year remediating subtraction — give Jose a calculator for it and pour his time into grade-level fractions, plus a problem-solving goal with a rubric Jose fills in himself. And then the last sentence of the whole book refuses to end cozy:

**Read aloud, slowly:**

> *“We can rethink disability, rethink mathematics, but we will also have to rethink our schools and systems to make them truly inclusive for all.”* — the last line of the book

Questions to raise

There’s a trade hiding inside MATHS. To make Jose’s goal measurable, Lambert had to shrink it — specific denominators, ninety percent accuracy, three trials — and whatever isn’t written into a goal tends not to get taught, because the goal is what’s legally accountable.

1.  **Can ‘ambitious’ and ‘measurable’ truly coexist in one goal, or does measurement always win in the end? And would you trust a student’s own self-assessment rubric, like Jose’s, as legal evidence of progress?**

2.  **Lambert says general-education math teachers usually feel ‘tangential’ to IEP goal-writing, yet the law requires them at the table. You’re the newest teacher in the room, and a goal like ‘identify one-digit numbers in a field of two’ comes up for a student you KNOW can do more. What exactly do you say? What do you bring with you?**
