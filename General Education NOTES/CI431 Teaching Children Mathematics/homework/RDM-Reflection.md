- Ch1

What touched me the most in this chapter is Sussan's "trust" in her students. She does not rush them, but instead gives them time to figure things out on their own. The image of Francisco immediately made me feel connected: he was sitting in his seat looking at his tablet, and his way of participating "looked wrong," but he was actually fully engaged in thinking. I was probably like that when I was in elementary school. Sussan did not correct him. Instead, she naturally accepted his way of learning. This kind of experience, where you are not treated as "a problem that needs to be fixed," is very rare and very precious. However, I do have one question: the book puts a lot of emphasis on waiting patiently, but some brains actually need a moderate external rhythm to stay motivated. Purely open waiting can sometimes make people lose focus instead. I fully agree with the idea of "trust," but the specific form it takes might need to be different for different people.

- Ch2

Luis was seen as a "top conceptual student" in an inquiry-based classroom, but after switching to a procedural teaching style, he became a "problem student," even though he himself never changed at all. As someone with Asperger's and ADHD, I deeply relate to the idea that "the environment decides whether you are 'capable' or 'disabled.'" I often do poorly when I need to speak up in class or pay attention to simple details, but I do much better in written, in-depth research. My question is: the author talks a lot about classrooms from kindergarten to eighth grade, but do these frameworks still apply to neurodivergent students at the college level? The way college math is assessed (timed exams and standardized test questions) seems to naturally create exactly the kind of "bizarre artificial constraint" that Dylan talks about, but nobody seems to care.

- Ch3

I heard the story in this chapter before from my calculus teacher in my first year of college: Edmund thought of himself as "slow," and he won not by speed in calculation but by visual intuition. It took him four years to solve a tiling problem. This is the complete opposite of the "fast means good" logic in schools. The math teachers I have had almost all believed that thinking faster means being smarter and having more mathematical talent and potential. What I related to the most was the part where mathematicians talked about memory: almost all of them could remember knowledge that has structure, but they could not remember isolated facts. This is especially true in introductory mathematical analysis courses at the college level, where algebraic knowledge is always easier to remember than analytical knowledge. However, math teaching in my hometown, whether in middle school or college, tends to focus on memorizing small, scattered problem-solving tricks, and exams and grades are almost entirely based on how many tricks you have memorized. This is also one of the main reasons I gave up continuing to study math.

- Ch4

The most valuable part of this chapter is that it breaks down the "inquiry or explicit instruction" debate from an either-or argument into a continuous spectrum. Mr. Jay's approach really convinced me: he used Visual Pattern Tasks to let students explore algebraic structures on their own first, and only after students had built up their intuition did he teach the rules, rather than doing it the other way around. The "strategic coaching" mentioned in the book is something I have rarely experienced in my own learning. Most of my teachers either let go completely or just gave the answer directly. What confused me is that the author points out that special education research and math education research have developed almost in parallel for decades. But since people cannot know before teaching which student is better suited for which style of education, could this parallel development of teaching research create a kind of unfairness?

- Ch5

The most valuable part of this chapter is that it presents the idea that "the problem is not in the student but in the design of the classroom" through a complete framework. Rita's story really stood out to me: she took a learning style quiz in sixth grade and was told she was "not a visual learner," and as a result she refused to draw pictures for two whole years. It was not until the author gently suggested she try it that she drew a picture and immediately understood the problem. Just one label blocked off an entire pathway of thinking that actually worked for her. The discussion about ADHD in this chapter also hit close to home for me: the 45-minute class period structure in schools happens to punish exactly what ADHD students are good at, which is deep focus, while rewarding exactly what they struggle with the most, which is switching between tasks frequently.

- Ch6

The story of Anthony in this chapter really stood out to me. Ms. Murphy thought Anthony did not like working in groups because he always argued with other students during group work. But after doing an empathy interview, Anthony said "I like working with partners, they help me with strategies," and his favorite part of class was actually solving problems together with his classmates. The teacher's understanding of him was completely the opposite of how he really felt. This reminded me of my own experience: I often stay silent during group discussions or interrupt others, and teachers almost always read this as "not cooperating" or "not interested," but the truth is I just do not know when it is my turn to speak. The power of empathy interviews as a tool is exactly in how simple they are: instead of assuming how students feel, you just ask them directly.

- Ch7

In this chapter, the story that stood out to me the most is Juan's. He did not want to do Counting Collections at first, and even when he did count, he did not care if he got a different number each time. But Hannah did not give up on him. She found a container he loved, and once counting became meaningful to him, he started to notice that getting 16 one time and 17 another time was a problem worth solving. This connects to earlier chapters about how the environment decides whether a student looks "capable." I also liked how Hannah moved smoothly between inquiry and explicit instruction depending on each student. The routine was the same for everyone, but the challenge was different. George skip counted by hundreds while Noemi worked with sets below 20, and nobody was singled out. One question I have is about Noemi walking away from the group share to count on her own. The author accepted this as engagement, but I wonder how teachers tell the difference between a student engaged in their own way and one who has truly lost focus.

- Ch8

What impressed me the most about Kayla's classroom in this chapter was how carefully she prepared students before they touched the math. She used a video, a discussion, and a "numberless problem" so students could understand the context before worrying about calculation. The chapter also argues against using "cognitive load" as an excuse to keep students with disabilities away from inquiry based math. The author says that as long as students understand the context and the action in the story, they can solve problems using whatever tools and languages work for them. I agree with this. In my own experience, what stopped me from doing well in math was never the difficulty itself, but the way problems were presented or the environment I was asked to work in. I also noticed how much choice Kayla gives students: number sets, tools, partners, and even which language to use.

\- Ch9

What stood out to me the most in this chapter is that Dina did not just hand her students a ruler. She gave them connecting cubes and asked them to measure the papers themselves, so they ended up building their own measuring sticks. I loved that Kamir, a student with dyslexia, was the one who made a stick color coded by groups of two, because counting in twos was easier for him. Instead of being told the "correct" tool, he found the structure that fit his own brain. This connects to something I have struggled with my whole life. When a method is forced on me, I freeze, but when I get to build my own way of organizing an idea, it finally sticks.

\- Ch10

The story that hit me the hardest in this chapter is Elijah's. During a fact quiz, his partner Bobby told him it did not count if he could not recall a fact quickly without using scratch paper. Elijah stood up for himself and said, "I know them all, just pressure." He clearly knew the facts, but being rushed and watched made it impossible to pull them out of memory. As someone with ADHD, I understand this feeling completely. I can know an idea deeply and still go blank the second a clock is running or someone is staring at me. This is one big reason I have always done worse on timed exams than on written work I can take my time with. I strongly agree with the author that timed tests mostly measure anxiety instead of understanding. The author also shows that reasoning strategies matter more than fast recall, yet almost every classroom I have seen still treats speed as the true sign of being smart at math.

\- Ch11

The student I connected with the most in this chapter is Inez. She always paid attention and always kept her eyes on the board, but she never shared answers and refused to use her fingers, most likely because she had been shamed for it in the past. Yola realized that Inez did not need help with content first. She needed help with engagement. Just by moving Inez next to her, chatting with her during turn and talks, and giving her a personal array to hold, Yola completely changed how much Inez took part. This meant a lot to me. I often stay silent during group work or speak at the wrong moment, and teachers usually read this as not caring, when the truth is I just do not know how to join in. Watching a teacher fix the conditions instead of blaming the student felt hopeful. I also related to Emmanuel, who understood big ideas easily but forgot the steps and strategies from one day to the next.

\- Ch12

What stayed with me the most in this chapter is how Ms. Rey described Oscar. His IEP labeled him as "nonverbal," but she simply called him "shy." Before he even started her class, she found out he loved space travel and brought him books about it, and their relationship grew from there. The same child that a legal document had written off as "nonverbal" was now talking and working hard, only because someone reached him through the thing he cared about. I know how much a single word in a file can follow you around and decide how people treat you before they even meet you.

\- Ch13

This was my favorite chapter so far, because Kit Golan is the first teacher in the book who is openly neurodivergent, and they built their entire approach out of their own ADHD. What hit me hardest was Kit describing how, even starting to say one sentence about a math problem can feel impossible, so a sentence frame gives you "permission to get started." I have never seen my own experience described so exactly. Getting started is the hardest part for me too, not the math itself. The sentence starters and the fixed steps of the routine are not there to limit thinking.

\- Ch14

This chapter felt like it was written about me. Jeremy knew how to multiply, but when the test started his legs were shaking and his page stayed blank, until the author let him use blocks and connect the problems to the "tower talk" they had practiced. Then he solved almost everything. The same boy went from looking like he knew nothing to knowing almost all of it, only because the format changed. The idea of the "affective drop," where anxiety takes up the working memory you need in order to think, finally gave a name to what happens to me on timed exams. I strongly agree that tools should count, because the author is right that tools are part of mathematics.

\- Ch15

Ending the book on IEP goals felt right to me, because this is the part of special education that students almost never get to see or shape. The story that stayed with me is Paulo Tan, a math professor, having to argue with a team who wanted his fifth-grade son's math goal to be "identify one-digit numbers in a field of two," when the boy was already far beyond that. If a parent who is literally a math expert had to fight that hard just to raise expectations, I cannot imagine how a family with less power would be heard. I also liked Jose, whose goal was rewritten from endless subtraction drills to grade-level fractions, with a calculator allowed for the subtraction he still found hard. That is the opposite of the low, deficit-based goals I usually saw growing up.
