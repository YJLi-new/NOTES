## Teaching for Conceptual Understanding

Understanding can be thought of as a continuum from instrumental understanding to relational understanding. These same ideas are also often described as procedural understanding and conceptual understanding.

- It moved from concrete representations to pictorial representations to abstract representations.
- It actively engaged students in sense making.
- It promoted student oral and written communication.
- It asked students to apply their knowledge to a new situation.

## Math Problems and Math Exercises

The class distinguished between a math “problem” and a math “exercise.” Depending on the type of lesson and the instructional goals, planning will look different.

| A Math Problem | A Math Exercise |
|:---|----|
| A mathematical task that requires students to think strategically, try out ideas, and engage in productive struggle in order to reach a solution. A problem is a situation in which we are not exactly sure of the steps to take and need to “figure something out.” | An exercise asks students to practice a skill they already have or almost have. Students have a set strategy or procedure to use on the set of questions. |

Knowing mathematics can mean many different things. The class connected math exercises and math problems to the five strands of mathematical proficiency from *Adding It Up*: procedural fluency, conceptual understanding, strategic competence, adaptive reasoning, and productive disposition.

## Two Main Lesson Structures

The class unpacked planning structures for two different types of math lessons.

### Task/Problem-Based Lessons

Task/problem-based lessons are more student centered. Their three major phases are:

- Launch, or before the task
- Explore, or during the task
- Summarize, or after the task

### Explicit Strategy Instruction

Explicit strategy instruction is more teacher centered. Its common phases are:

- I Do: teacher modeling
- We Do: scaffolding and supported practice
- You Do: individual practice

## The Three Phases of Problem-Based Lessons

A problem-based lesson uses three phases:

1.  Before (Launch): The teacher readies students for the task.
2.  During (Explore): Students problem-solve with support.
3.  After (Summarize): The teacher guides discussion and solidifies the main mathematical ideas.

The class used the video “A Problem-Based Lesson: A Table for 22” as an example of teaching through problem solving. The video link was: https://vimeo.com/46127286.

## Teacher Actions in Problem-Based Lessons

### Before: Launch

During the launch, the teacher can:

- Activate prior knowledge.
- Make sure the problem is understood, including the context, directions, and goal.
- Set clear expectations for how work is to be done, with whom, what the end product is, and how groups will operate.
- Pique interest and curiosity.
- Make resources available.
- Sometimes solve a shorter, related problem.

### During: Explore

During exploration, the teacher can:

- “Let go” and give students space to work.
- Circulate and listen.
- Ask questions to check for understanding.
- Scaffold when needed.
- Provide a work organizer if it is helpful.
- Look for ideas to share later.
- Encourage students to solve problems in more than one way.
- Provide extension questions for early finishers.

### After: Summarize

During the summary, the teacher can:

- Facilitate discussion.
- Strategically ask certain students to share ideas, strategies, and solutions.
- Display student work.
- Summarize and solidify the key ideas being learned.
- Introduce emergent vocabulary.
- Ask questions to check for understanding and push on the main ideas.
- Avoid being the sole judge of “right” and “wrong.”
- Ask the class whether an idea makes sense.

## Benefits and Challenges of Teaching Through Problem Solving

Teaching through problem solving has many potential benefits. It:

- Focuses students’ attention on ideas and sense making.
- Builds on students’ strengths.
- Allows for extensions and elaborations.
- Provides formative assessment data.
- Engages students.
- Invites creativity.
- Develops student confidence and mathematical identities.

The class also asked students to consider potential challenges of teaching through problem solving.

## Explicit Strategy Instruction: I Do, We Do, You Do

Explicit strategy instruction can be described as a sequence of teacher modeling, scaffolded practice, and independent work. In the video example used in class, the phases were expanded into four parts:

1.  I do it alone.
2.  We do it together.
3.  You do it together.
4.  You do it alone.

## Teacher Actions in Explicit Strategy Instruction

### I Do: Teacher Modeling

During teacher modeling, the teacher can:

- Pique interest with relevant questions and contexts.
- Create a need for the new procedure or strategy.
- Model the thinking process aloud and illuminate the decision-making along the way.
- Use visual representations and manipulatives.
- Ask questions to keep students engaged.
- Guide students using direct questions.

### We Do: Scaffolded Practice

During scaffolded practice, the teacher can:

- Ask prompting questions to see whether students know what to do next and why.
- Ask one question at a time and allow students to try with a partner or group.
- Address common mistakes and sticky points as they arise.
- Allow students to use visuals and manipulatives.
- Keep the number of questions limited and allow students to check whether answers are correct right away.

### You Do: Independent Practice

During independent practice, the teacher can:

- Give students opportunities to try questions without initial help from the teacher or peers.
- Allow students to check answers quickly so they can learn from mistakes.
- Allow students to use visuals and manipulatives.
- Offer extra support for students who still need it, and set up a next activity for students who are ready.

## Bringing Both Structures Together

The class asked: What role does each format play in a math classroom? If a teacher used only one of these two methods all year for every lesson, what would students miss?

Pamela Seda and Kyndall Brown’s book *Choosing to See: A Framework for Equity in the Math Classroom* was referenced. They argue for being intentional about honoring and building upon students’ prior knowledge and experiences. Starting instruction with teacher modeling, or “I do,” can reinforce the idea that only the teacher’s knowledge matters and can promote passive learning rather than empowered learning. Dr. Seda recommends combining the two lesson structures rather than relying on only one.

## Game: Shut the Box

The class began with the game *Shut the Box*.

Materials needed:

- 2-4 players
- Two dice
- A game board
- Writing utensils

Goal: Have the lowest score at the end of the game.

To play:

1.  The first player rolls both dice and crosses out numbers on their edge of the board whose total is the same as the total number of dots on the dice. For example, if a player rolls 8, they can cross out 8, or 5 and 3, or 6 and 2, or 1 and 2 and 5.
2.  Players continue taking turns, rolling and crossing out numbers.
3.  If a player gets stuck and cannot make the number rolled, that player is finished for that game. The game continues until each player is stuck.
4.  Each player adds up the numbers on their edge of the board that have not been crossed out.
5.  The player with the lowest total wins.

After playing, students put on their “teacher hats” and considered:

- Why might teachers play *Shut the Box* with their students?
- What mathematical skills does it elicit?
- What might be important considerations?

## From Lesson Structures to Student Needs

## Differentiation

Students were asked: How would you explain what “differentiation” means to someone who is unfamiliar with this topic in education?

The class highlighted four areas where differentiation can occur:

### Content

Students may have individualized math content goals or be given different levels of math work depending on their readiness.

### Process

Students may receive access to specific learning manipulatives or resources. They may receive supports such as scaffolded directions, visual directions, calculator use, or extra one-on-one attention.

### Product

Students might receive choices in how they show what they know. Some students may be able to explain better than they write, or show better than they tell. There may also be project options with different choices of a final product.

### Learning Environment

Teachers may use flexible grouping to decide the best ways to form groups for a variety of reasons, such as interests, readiness, personalities, and product choices. Some students may also need quieter environments.

## Accommodations and Modifications

Students were asked how they would explain the difference between accommodations and modifications.

A key distinction from the slides was:

- Accommodations level the playing field.
- Modifications change the field the student is playing on.

In other words, accommodations change how a student learns or accesses the material, while modifications often change what a student is taught or expected to learn. The class considered which examples would most likely be a modification, an accommodation, or something that could be either.

The slide classified the differentiation areas as follows:

- Content: most likely a modification.
- Process: most likely an accommodation.
- Product: could be either.
- Learning environment: most likely an accommodation.

## Response to Intervention (RTI)

Chapter 6 introduced Response to Intervention, or RTI, as a common system of school support. The RTI triangle was discussed using three tiers:

| Tier | Approximate Percentage | Description |
|:---|----|----|
| Tier 1 | 80-90% | Support for all students through high-quality curriculum, research-based teaching practices, and ongoing progress monitoring. |
| Tier 2 | 5-10% | Supplemental targeted instruction, often in small groups, using interventions that address key math concepts, reinforce ideas with more visuals and manipulatives, and provide more supportive and precise prompts. This is often done with another qualified educator. |
| Tier 3 | 1-5% | Individualized support. Students are usually referred to work one-on-one with a special educator, who may decide whether to evaluate for special education services. |

The class asked students to reflect on their prior knowledge and experiences with RTI and to interpret what the triangle means.

These groups are not mutually exclusive. A student may belong to more than one group at the same time.

## Number Sense

The class asked: What is number sense?

Number sense was defined using Van de Walle’s description:

> “good intuition about numbers and their relationships”

It develops gradually through exploring numbers, visualizing them in many contexts, and relating them in ways that are not limited by traditional algorithms.

## Components of Number Sense

The class reviewed six major components of number sense:

1.  Subitizing
2.  Flexible counting
3.  More than, less than, and equal to
4.  Benchmark numbers
5.  Part-part-whole relationships
6.  Place value concepts

## Subitizing and Dot Talks

Subitizing means seeing a group of objects and knowing how many there are without counting.

“Dot Talks” develop the skill of subitizing. A Dot Talk can follow this structure:

1.  Flash an image for just a few seconds.
2.  Ask students, “How many did you see?”
3.  Ask, “How could you tell how many there were?”
4.  Let students talk with a partner.
5.  Show the image again when discussing as a whole class.

The class also viewed a kindergarten example using a magnet board and a ten-frame.

## Flexible Counting

Flexible counting includes:

- Counting on
- Counting back
- Skip counting

## More Than, Less Than, and Same

This component involves:

- Making comparisons
- Constructing sets
- Making choices

## Benchmark Numbers of 5 and 10

The number 10 is key in our numeration system, and two 5s equal 10. Therefore, understanding the relationship between a number, especially a number from 1 to 10, and the benchmarks 5 and 10 is very useful.

Examples:

- If I have 4, I need 1 more to get to 5.
- If I have 8, I need 2 more to get to 10.

Five-frames and ten-frames were presented as tools for helping students see and reason with these benchmarks.

## Part-Part-Whole Relationships

Part-part-whole relationships involve conceptualizing a number as being composed of two or more parts.

Examples:

- 7 can be a set of 3 and a set of 4, or a set of 5 and a set of 2.
- 9 can be 3 sets of 3, or a set of 6 and a set of 3.

The visual slide included part-whole diagrams, bar models, ten-frame representations, and physical manipulatives as ways to represent this relationship.

## Place Value Concepts

Recognizing a set of 10 is a major part of understanding numbers between 10 and 20.

Examples:

- 10 is a bundle of 10 ones.
- A bundle of 10 and 6 more is 16.

The visual examples included bundled sticks, linking cubes, and a place value mat that organized cubes into tens and ones.

## Operation Sense

After number sense, the class moved to operation sense. Operation sense involves:

- Understanding the meanings of the operations.
- Understanding the relationships between the operations.
- Having the ability to apply the operations flexibly to a range of problem-solving contexts.

Young children have intuitive problem-solving ideas. Their ideas are often grounded in context and meaning: What is happening? This question is also how we categorize different types of addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division problems.

## Addition and Subtraction Meanings

Students solved and acted out problems such as:

1.  Julie had 9 stuffed animals. Her friends gave her 6 more for her birthday. How many does she have now?
2.  Julie had 9 stuffed animals. Her friend gave her some more. Now she has 15. How many did her friend give her?
3.  Julie had some stuffed animals. Her friend took 6. Now she has 9. How many did she have at first?

Students acted out the problems, used cubes to solve them, and wrote the number sentence or equation represented by each problem.

## Addition and Subtraction Structures

The class connected Common Core language to Van de Walle’s structures:

| Common Core Language | Van de Walle Structure | Meaning |
|:---|----|----|
| Adding To | Join | An action increases the initial amount. |
| Taking From | Separate | An action decreases the initial amount. |
| Putting Together and Taking Apart | Part-Part-Whole | There is one set with distinct components. |
| Comparing | Comparison | There are two sets of different quantities. |

There are different structures, and there are also different unknowns.

Examples:

- Join, unknown change: Chloe has 2 squeaky toys and Kingsley brings her some more. Now she has 5 toys. How many toys did Kingsley bring her?
- Separate, unknown initial: Chloe had some squeaky toys. Kingsley stole 2. Now Chloe has 6 tennis toys. How many toys did Chloe have to begin with?
- Part-part-whole, unknown part: Chloe has two types of squeaky toys, birds and alligators. She has 9 toys in all. If 5 of the toys are squeaky alligators, how many bird toys does she have?
- Comparison, unknown smaller set: Chloe has 8 squeaky toys. Kingsley has 3 less toys than Chloe. How many toys does Kingsley have?

A group challenge asked students to label problems by structure and by unknown, such as comparison with unknown small set, join with unknown change, part-part-whole with unknown part, and separate with unknown result.

The class then watched Aaliyah problem-solve. For each video, students identified the problem type and the unknown.

## A Related First-Grade Common Core Standard

## Multiplication and Division Meanings

Students then moved to multiplication and division and solved problems such as:

1.  The pet store had 5 bird cages with 3 birds in each cage. How many total birds do they have?
2.  There are 15 birds. The pet store wants to split the birds into groups of 3 birds each. How many groups will they have?
3.  There are 15 birds. The pet store wants to split them evenly into 3 cages. How many birds will go in each cage?

Students modeled the problems with cubes and wrote the corresponding number sentences.

## Multiplication and Division Structures

The selected multiplication and division structures were:

| Structure | Vocabulary or Equation |
|:---|----|
| Equal Groups | number of groups x group size = product |
| Arrays | number of rows x number in each row = product; this is also a form of number of groups x group size = product |
| Area | length x width = area |
| Comparison | group size x multiplier = product; a sample multiplier might be “three times more” |

The class emphasized “groups language” and compared:

- 5 groups of 3: 5 x 3 = 15, where number of groups x group size = total.
- 3 groups of 5: 3 x 5 = 15, where number of groups x group size = total.

## Division Types: Partition and Measurement

Students compared two division problems:

- Pablo had 20 crackers. He made them into stacks that had 4 crackers in each stack. How many stacks did he make?
- Pablo had 20 crackers. He put them equally into 4 bags. How many were in each bag?

Both can be represented by 20 ÷ 4 = ?, but the unknown is different.

| Type of Division     | Other Name           | Unknown                  |
|:---------------------|----------------------|--------------------------|
| Measurement Division | Repeated Subtraction | Unknown number of groups |
| Partition Division   | Fair Sharing         | Unknown group size       |

Students also checked their understanding with this problem:

“June made 12 cookies. She placed them equally into 4 baggies. How many were in each bag?”

This was identified as an equal groups problem with unknown group size: 4 x ? = 12.

## Sample Third-Grade CCSS Standards

The class displayed sample third-grade Common Core standards for multiplication and division. Key standards included:

- CC.3.OA.1: Represent and solve problems involving multiplication and division. Interpret products of whole numbers, such as interpreting 5 x 7 as the total number of objects in 5 groups of 7 objects each. Describe a context in which a total number of objects can be expressed as 5 x 7.
- CC.3.OA.2: Represent and solve problems involving multiplication and division. Interpret whole-number quotients, such as interpreting 56 ÷ 8 as the number of objects in each share when 56 objects are partitioned equally into 8 shares, or as the number of shares when 56 objects are partitioned into equal shares of 8 objects each. Describe a context in which a number of shares or groups can be expressed as 56 ÷ 8.
- CC.3.OA.3: Use multiplication and division within 100 to solve word problems in situations involving equal groups, arrays, and measurement quantities. Students can use drawings and equations with a symbol for the unknown number to represent the problem.
- CC.3.OA.4: Determine the unknown whole number in a multiplication or division equation relating three whole numbers, such as 8 x ? = 48, 5 = \_\_ ÷ 3, and 6 x 6 = ?.

## Closing Thoughts

The class closed with several important ideas:

> “The tendency in the United States is to have students solve many problems in a single lesson with a focus on getting the answers. But if you change the focus to sense making, solving only a few problems using multiple representations strategically such as physical materials, drawings, as well as equations can be a better approach.” (p. 169)

Teachers should use accurate language. Addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division are related and should be taught as related. Teachers should not wait for students to “master” one operation before moving on to another.

# Class 5: Basic Facts and Basic Fact Fluency

## Number Talks

Class 5 began with a Number Talk.

Number Talks are brief math conversations where students solve mentally, share strategies, and learn from one another’s thinking. Rather than emphasizing one “right” method, Number Talks help students build:

- Mental math fluency
- Flexible thinking
- Mathematical reasoning
- Confidence explaining ideas

The teacher’s role is to record student thinking, ask clarifying questions, and help the class notice patterns, connections, and efficient strategies.

## Steps to a Number Talk

A Number Talk can follow these steps:

1.  The teacher sets the expectations:
    - Students solve the problem mentally.
    - Students put a thumb up quietly by their chest when they have a solution, and use more fingers to signal that they have more solutions.
2.  The teacher reveals the problem.
3.  Students solve the problem.
4.  The teacher collects answers on the board.
5.  The teacher collects solution strategies on the board.
6.  The teacher sums up the conversation and makes connections between big ideas.

## What Are Basic Facts?

For addition and subtraction, basic facts involve two addends that are both less than 10. Examples include:

- 3 + 8 = 11
- 15 - 6 = 9

For multiplication and division, basic facts involve two factors that are both less than 10. Examples include:

- 5 x 9 = 45
- 36 ÷ 4 = 9

Students reflected on their own memories and experiences with learning basic facts.

## Big Goal: Fluency

The major goal is fluency. Van de Walle emphasizes:

> “Fluency is not only about speed; it includes being able to flexibly, accurately, efficiently, and appropriately solve problems.”

Basic fact fluency develops through three phases:

1.  Counting
2.  Reasoning strategies

## Foundational Fact Sets for Addition and Subtraction

Certain addition facts are foundational for most addition and subtraction reasoning strategies. These foundational facts do not need to be learned by rote memorization; they can be retained through meaningful exposure to activities and stories over time.

### One More and Two More

+1 means “one more.” One more is the same as counting on to the next number.

Examples:

- 6 + 1 is the next number after 6, or 7.
- 1 + 4 is the next number after 4, or 5.

+2 means “two more.” Two more is the same as counting on two numbers ahead, or “the next next number.”

Examples:

- 6 + 2 is the second number after 6, or 8.
- 2 + 4 is the second number after 4, or 6.

### Doubles Facts

Students tend to grasp doubles more easily than many other facts. Examples include 5 + 5, 6 + 6, and 8 + 8. A sample doubles story was:

“Bre and Soren each found 7 pennies on the playground. How many did they find altogether?”

### Combinations to Ten

Combinations to ten ask: “How many more to make ten?” These facts can be practiced with tasks such as Sleeping Birds, Covered Parts, ten-frame work, and the game “Shoot the Orbs” at https://www.mathplayground.com/number_bonds_20.html.

## Addition and Subtraction Reasoning Strategies

Once students have gained proficiency with foundational facts, they are more ready to apply them using reasoning strategies. Reasoning strategies help students make sense of their basic facts and are an important step on the way to mastery.

### Near Doubles

When students know their doubles, they can use near doubles.

Example: 5 + 6

“I know 5 + 5 is 10, and 6 is 1 more than 5, so 5 + 6 must be 11.”

Example: 8 + 7

“I know 8 + 8 is 16, and 7 is one less than 8, so 8 + 7 must be 15.”

This can be described as doubles-plus-one or doubles-minus-one. Students considered how doubles could help with 6 + 7, 9 + 8, or 5 + 7.

### Using Tens

The class examined several strategies that use 10 as a benchmark:

- Making 10
- Pretend-a-10, or use 10
- Down under 10
- Take from 10
- Think addition

Examples:

- Making 10 for 6 + 8: “I know 8 is 2 away from 10, so I take 2 away from the 6 and add it to the 8 to make 10. Then I have 10 + 4, which equals 14.”
- Pretend-a-10 for 9 + 6: “I start with a 10 fact. 10 + 6 is 16, and 9 is one less than 10, so the sum is 1 less than 16, or 15.”
- Down under 10 for 14 - 8: “I jump back 4 to get to 10, and then I still need to jump back 4 more. I end on 6.”
- Take from 10 for 15 - 8: “I know 15 is 10 + 5. I know 10 - 8 is 2. If I add 5 back to 2, I get 7.”
- Think addition for 15 - 8: “I know that 8 plus something equals 15. I need 7 more to get to 15.”

The class summarized these strategies as:

- Addition: near doubles, make 10, pretend-a-10.
- Subtraction: think addition, down under 10, take from 10.

Students practiced showing several strategies for 6 + 7 and 13 - 6.

## Multiplication Strategy: Break Apart

The break-apart strategy decomposes one factor to use facts students already know.

Examples:

- To solve 8 x 3, break 3 into 2 + 1: 8 x 2 = 16 and 8 x 1 = 8, so 16 + 8 = 24.
- To solve 7 x 4, break 4 into 2 + 2: 7 x 2 = 14 and 7 x 2 = 14, so 14 + 14 = 28. This uses doubles.
- To solve 9 x 4, use 10 groups of 4 minus 1 group of 4: 10 x 4 = 40 and 40 - 4 = 36.

## Multiplication Reasoning: Adding or Subtracting a Group

Students reasoned about facts by adding or subtracting a group.

Examples:

- All 3 facts can be thought of as x2 and one more group. For example, 7 x 3 is 7 x 2 and one more 7: 14 + 7 = 21.
- All 6 facts can be thought of as x5 and one more group. For example, 4 x 6 is 4 x 5 and one more 4: 20 + 4 = 24.
- All 9 facts can be thought of as x10 and one less group. For example, 7 x 9 is 7 x 10 and one less 7: 70 - 7 = 63.

Students practiced reasoning strategies for 3 x 9 and 7 x 6.

## Division Strategy: Think Multiplication

The division strategy “think multiplication” asks students to connect division to multiplication.

Example:

- What is 36 ÷ 4?
- Ask: 4 times what is 36?
- Then apply a multiplication strategy.

## Grade-Level Fluency Standards

The class displayed Common Core standards related to basic fact fluency and reasoning.

### First Grade

First-grade expectations include adding and subtracting within 20, demonstrating fluency for addition and subtraction within 10. Students should use strategies such as counting on, making ten, decomposing a number leading to a ten, using the relationship between addition and subtraction, and creating equivalent but easier or known sums.

### Second Grade

Second-grade expectations include using addition and subtraction within 100 to solve one- and two-step word problems, including situations of adding to, taking from, putting together, taking apart, and comparing, with unknowns in all positions.

Students should fluently add and subtract within 20 using mental strategies. By the end of Grade 2, students should know from memory all sums of two one-digit numbers.

Second grade also includes foundational work for multiplication: determining whether a group of objects up to 20 has an odd or even number of members, pairing objects or counting by 2s, writing an equation for an even number as a sum of two equal addends, and using addition to find totals in rectangular arrays with up to 5 rows and 5 columns.

### Third Grade

Third-grade expectations include interpreting products of whole numbers, interpreting whole-number quotients, solving multiplication and division word problems involving equal groups, arrays, and measurement quantities, and determining unknown numbers in multiplication or division equations.

Third grade also includes applying properties of operations as strategies to multiply and divide, understanding division as an unknown-factor problem, and fluently multiplying and dividing within 100 using strategies based on the relationship between multiplication and division and properties of operations. By the end of Grade 3, students should know from memory all products of two one-digit numbers.

## What Is Place Value?

Place value is a system that allows us to represent and understand quantities that are larger than a single digit.

Having place value means that the position, or place, of a digit defines its value. Place value helps us differentiate between numbers such as 91 and 19. Students were asked:

- What is the value of the 1 and the 9 in each number?
- How do you know?

## Place Value Is Not the Same as Base 10

Place value is not identical to Base 10, but the two are related.

The class revisited Zork, who used a Base 4 system with 16s, 4s, and 1s. Zork also used place value to write numbers. Students considered:

- What is the value of each 2 in 222_4?
- What is the value of each 2 in 222_10?

Base systems describe how we group numbers. In Base 10, we group by 10,000s, 1,000s, 100s, 10s, and 1s. Place value describes how we record and represent those groupings.

## Spoken and Written Language

Spoken and written language are not the same. In spoken language, we usually name the groupings explicitly. For example, we say “three hundred forty two.”

In written language, we do not write 300402. We write 342. Place value tells us that 342 means:

300 + 40 + 2

## Base-Ten Language, Standard Language, Expanded Form, and Common Notation

## Place Value Across Grade Levels

The class reviewed a progression of Common Core standards related to place value.

### Kindergarten

Kindergarten students work with numbers 11-19 to gain foundations for place value. They compose and decompose numbers from 11 to 19 into ten ones and some further ones, using objects or drawings and recording each composition or decomposition by a drawing or equation such as 18 = 10 + 8. They understand that these numbers are composed of ten ones and one, two, three, four, five, six, seven, eight, or nine ones.

### First Grade

First-grade students extend the counting sequence by counting to 120, starting at any number less than 120. They read and write numerals and represent a number of objects with a written numeral.

They understand that the two digits of a two-digit number represent amounts of tens and ones. Special cases include:

- 10 can be thought of as a bundle of ten ones called a “ten.”
- Numbers from 11 to 19 are composed of a ten and one, two, three, four, five, six, seven, eight, or nine ones.
- The numbers 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, 60, 70, 80, and 90 refer to one through nine tens and 0 ones.

First graders compare two two-digit numbers based on the meanings of the tens and ones digits, recording comparisons with \>, =, and \<.

They also use place value understanding and properties of operations to add and subtract. They add within 100, including adding a two-digit number and a one-digit number and adding a two-digit number and a multiple of 10. They use concrete models or drawings and strategies based on place value, properties of operations, and/or the relationship between addition and subtraction. They relate strategies to written methods and explain the reasoning used. They understand that when adding two-digit numbers, tens are added to tens, ones are added to ones, and sometimes it is necessary to compose a ten.

They mentally find 10 more or 10 less than a two-digit number without counting, and they subtract multiples of 10 in the range 10-90 from multiples of 10 in the range 10-90 using concrete models, drawings, and place-value-based strategies.

### Second Grade

Second-grade students understand that the three digits of a three-digit number represent amounts of hundreds, tens, and ones. For example, 706 equals 7 hundreds, 0 tens, and 6 ones.

They understand that 100 can be thought of as a bundle of ten tens called a “hundred,” and that the numbers 100, 200, 300, 400, 500, 600, 700, 800, and 900 refer to one through nine hundreds with 0 tens and 0 ones.

They count within 1,000 and skip-count by 5s, 10s, and 100s. They read and write numbers to 1,000 using base-ten numerals, number names, and expanded form. They compare two three-digit numbers based on the meanings of the hundreds, tens, and ones digits.

They fluently add and subtract within 100 using strategies based on place value, properties of operations, and/or the relationship between addition and subtraction. They add up to four two-digit numbers using strategies based on place value and properties of operations. They also add and subtract within 1,000 using concrete models or drawings and strategies based on place value.

### Third Grade

Third-grade students use place value understanding and properties of operations to perform multi-digit arithmetic. They use place value understanding to round whole numbers to the nearest 10 or 100.

They fluently add and subtract within 1,000 using strategies and algorithms based on place value, properties of operations, and/or the relationship between addition and subtraction. They also multiply one-digit whole numbers by multiples of 10 in the range 10-90, such as 9 x 80 or 5 x 60, using strategies based on place value and properties of operations.

### Fourth Grade

Fourth-grade students generalize place value understanding for multi-digit whole numbers. They recognize that in a multi-digit whole number, a digit in one place represents ten times what it represents in the place to its right. For example, they recognize that 700 ÷ 70 = 10 by applying concepts of place value and division.

They read and write multi-digit whole numbers using base-ten numerals, number names, and expanded form, and they compare multi-digit numbers based on the meanings of the digits in each place. They use place value understanding to round multi-digit whole numbers to any place.

They also fluently add and subtract multi-digit whole numbers using the standard algorithm, multiply whole numbers using strategies based on place value and properties of operations, and find whole-number quotients and remainders using strategies based on place value, properties of operations, and/or the relationship between multiplication and division.

### Fifth Grade

Fifth-grade students understand the place value system. They recognize that in a multi-digit number, a digit in one place represents 10 times as much as it represents in the place to its right and 1/10 of what it represents in the place to its left.

They explain patterns in the number of zeros of a product when multiplying by powers of 10, and they explain patterns in the placement of the decimal point when multiplying or dividing by a power of 10. They use whole-number exponents to denote powers of 10.

They read, write, and compare decimals to thousandths. They read and write decimals to thousandths using base-ten numerals, number names, and expanded form. For example:

347.392 = 3 x 100 + 4 x 10 + 7 x 1 + 3 x (1/10) + 9 x (1/100) + 2 x (1/1000)

They use place value understanding to round decimals to any place.

They perform operations with multi-digit whole numbers and decimals to hundredths. They fluently multiply multi-digit whole numbers using the standard algorithm, find whole-number quotients using strategies based on place value and properties of operations, and add, subtract, multiply, and divide decimals to hundredths using concrete models or drawings and strategies based on place value.

## Multi-Digit Computation

The class then moved to multi-digit computation. The course had already discussed developing number sense, basic fact computation, and place value understanding. These are the building blocks for multi-digit computation.

## Development of Computational Strategies

The development of computational strategies can be described in three broad categories:

1.  Direct Modeling
    - Count by ones.
    - Use base-ten models.
2.  Reasoning Strategies
    - Use properties and place value.
    - Use mental methods or written records when needed.
3.  Standard Algorithms
    - Follow a series of steps.
    - Use representations to model the steps.

## Multi-Digit Addition

### Addition Problem Context

The addition example was:

“A group of people were on an airplane. There were 57 adults and 45 children. How many people were on the plane?”

### Addition: Direct Modeling

Direct modeling uses manipulatives or drawings along with counting to directly represent what is happening in the problem.

For 57 + 45, one direct modeling approach is:

1.  50 + 40 = 90
2.  7 + 5 = 12
3.  90 + 12 = 102

Another approach is to count by 10s to 90 and then count up from there to 102.

A place value mat can help students organize the tens and ones. The total is 102 people.

### Addition: Reasoning Strategies

Addition reasoning strategies use flexible computation methods that apply a person’s understanding of place value and the properties of operations.

Van de Walle describes many benefits to supporting students’ use of reasoning strategies. The class discussed three broad categories:

- Jump strategies
- Decomposition strategies
- Compensation strategies

Students considered what these strategies might look like for 57 + 45.

### Addition Reasoning: Jump Strategies

Jump strategies involve “jumping” or counting on from one addend to the sum. Tools can include a hundreds chart or an empty/open number line.

The class asked how a blank line can help with problem solving and practiced using an open number line together.

### Addition Reasoning: Decomposition Strategies

Decomposition strategies involve partitioning or breaking apart one or more numbers, often into place value parts, to make the addition easier.

Example: Add tens, add ones, then combine.

57 + 45:

- 50 + 40 = 90
- 7 + 5 = 12
- 90 + 12 = 102

Example: Add on tens, then add ones.

57 + 45:

- 57 and 40 more is 97.
- Then add the other 5.
- 97 and 3 is 100, and 2 more is 102.

Example: Move some to make tens.

57 + 45:

- Take 3 from the 45 and put it with 57 to make 60.
- Now the problem is 60 + 42.
- 60 + 42 = 102.

### Addition Reasoning: Compensation Strategies

Compensation strategies flexibly change the numbers to make them easier to work with and then adjust the sum at the end to account for the initial change.

Examples for 57 + 45:

- 57 + 50 = 107. That is 5 extra, so 107 - 5 = 102.
- 60 + 45 = 105. That is 3 extra, so 105 - 3 = 102.
- 60 + 50 = 110. That is 8 extra, so 110 - 8 = 102.

### Addition: Standard Algorithm

The standard algorithm is reliable and efficient if students understand why and how it works. The class talked through the algorithm for 57 + 45 using base-ten language and place value reasoning.

### Try It: Addition

With a partner, students solved this problem:

“On the first day of class registration, 48 students registered for the class. Later, 36 more students signed up. In total, how many students registered?”

They solved it using:

1.  Direct modeling.
2.  Three different reasoning strategies: jump, decomposition, and compensation.
3.  The standard algorithm, talking through the steps using base-ten language and possibly a place value chart.

## Multi-Digit Subtraction

### Subtraction Problem Context

The subtraction example was:

“Walter had 45 songs on his cell phone. His little brother accidentally deleted 27 of them. How many songs does Walter have left?”

### Subtraction: Direct Modeling

Direct modeling uses manipulatives or drawings along with counting to directly represent what is happening. With base-ten blocks, students can model 45 and remove 27. The answer is 18.

The class also discussed a possible issue with student representations. Sometimes students model a subtraction equation in a way that does not correctly represent the initial amount, the amount taken away, or the remaining amount. Teachers should attend to whether the representation actually matches the story context and equation.

### Subtraction Reasoning Strategies

As with addition, subtraction reasoning strategies can include:

- Jump strategies
- Decomposition strategies
- Compensation strategies

Students considered what these strategies might look like for 45 - 27.

### Subtraction: Jump Strategies

Jump strategies for subtraction can involve jumping forward, or “think addition,” or jumping backward, or “take away.” Students can use a hundreds chart or an empty/open number line.

Examples for 45 - 27:

**Add tens to get close, then ones**

- 27 + 10 = 37.
- 20 more would be too much.
- 3 more gets to 40, and 5 more gets to 45.
- 10 + 3 + 5 = 18.

**Add tens to overshoot, then come back**

- 27 + 20 = 47.
- That is 2 beyond 45.
- 2 less than 20 is 18.

**Add ones to make a ten, then tens and ones**

- 27 + 3 = 30.
- 30 + 15 = 45.
- 3 + 15 = 18.

### Subtraction: Decomposition Strategies

Decomposition strategies break numbers apart to make the subtraction easier.

Examples for 45 - 27:

**Take tens from the tens, then subtract ones**

- 40 - 20 = 20.
- Take away 7 more to get 13.
- Add 5 back to get 18.

**Take away tens, then ones**

- 45 - 20 = 25.
- Then take away 5 to get 20.
- Then take away 2 to get 18.

### Subtraction: Compensation Strategies

Compensation strategies adjust one or both numbers to make the subtraction easier, then compensate.

Examples for 45 - 27:

**Take extra tens, then add back**

- 45 - 30 = 15.
- That took 3 too many, so add 3 back.
- 15 + 3 = 18.

**Add to the whole if necessary**

- Give 2 to 45 to make 47.
- 47 - 27 = 20.
- Take back the extra 2.
- 20 - 2 = 18.

**Use a nice number and compensate**

- 27 + 3 = 30.
- 45 - 30 = 15.
- 15 + 3 = 18.

Or:

- 45 + 5 = 50.
- 50 - 27 = 23.
- 23 - 5 = 18.

### Subtraction: Standard Algorithm

The standard algorithm is reliable and efficient if students understand why and how it works. The class talked through the algorithm for 45 - 27 using base-ten language.

### Subtraction with Zeros

Subtraction becomes especially interesting when many zeros are involved. Students solved mentally:

- 1000 - 6 = ?

They were also asked whether they could solve it using the standard algorithm and what makes this type of problem hard.

One strategy for dealing with lots of zeros is to take away 1 from both numbers. Students tried this with:

- 20,000 - 372 = ?

## Algorithms Are Cultural

The class emphasized that algorithms are cultural. What is called the “standard algorithm” is not standard everywhere. The equal-addition subtraction algorithm is common in many Latin American countries.

The major takeaway was:

What we call “standard” is no better than what anyone else calls standard.

## Key Takeaways

Key takeaways from the class included:

- Being able to compute with multi-digit numbers is all about being able to compose and decompose numbers; doing so makes numbers easier to work with.
- Students should be able to think flexibly and choose problem-solving strategies that make sense for the specific problem context.
- Standard algorithms can be elegant, but when they are taught without strong conceptual understanding first, they can cause students to think in terms of isolated digits and lose track of place value.
- For mental computation, standard algorithms usually are not the most efficient or easy-to-use tools.
